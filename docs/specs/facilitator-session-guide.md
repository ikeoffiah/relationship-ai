# The facilitator session guide

Owner: product/design (`local_81faf803`). Previously unowned.

*Originally justified as a Cohort License deliverable. That SKU died with the
one-SKU pricing decision (`go-to-market.md` §5). The guide survives it on its own
merits: it is what makes report page 5 teachable rather than merely printed, and
under D3.6a a slot-in kit needs it more, not less.*

**One document, identical for every cohort. It contains no couple's data** — that
is the §2.1 rule that keeps the report symmetric, and this guide is the reason
that rule costs a facilitator nothing.

Per D3.6a, facilitators are expected to want *materials that slot into a
programme they must already run*, not a programme to replace it. So §3 gives a
full session **and** four standalone twenty-minute drop-ins.

## Draft — the page itself

> # Running a session from the Bliss report
>
> Each couple in your cohort has a report. **They have it too — the same eight
> pages you do.** Nothing in it is hidden from them, and there is no version with
> extra notes for you. That is deliberate: you can read from it out loud without
> anyone wondering what else it says.
>
> ---
>
> ## Before you start
>
> **Read page 5 first.** Pages 2–4 describe the couple; page 5 is what you teach.
> Everything else is context for it.
>
> **You will not see their answers**, only what the report makes of them. If you
> want to know how someone answered something, ask them in the room — that
> conversation is worth more than the data would have been.
>
> **Have them bring their own copy.** On paper if you can. Page 7 is blank for
> notes and the couples who write on it are the ones who still have it a year
> later.
>
> ---
>
> ## The shape of ninety minutes
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **10 min** | Page 1 together. Read "How to read this" out loud — especially that there is no score, and that the report can be wrong. Say the last part yourself; it gives them permission to disagree, and a couple who correct the report are engaged rather than difficult. |
> | **15 min** | Pages 2 and 3. Each partner reads **their own** page, then tells the other one thing that landed and one thing that missed. Do not have them read each other's page aloud — let each speak for themselves. |
> | **15 min** | Page 4, "Where you meet". Read it together. The question to ask afterwards: *"Which part of that already sounds familiar?"* |
> | **40 min** | Page 5. One conversation at a time, roughly ten minutes each. You will not finish all four properly — that is fine, and §"If you only have twenty minutes" below is how to use the rest later. |
> | **10 min** | Page 6, side by side, and page 7. Let them write. Silence here is working time, not a gap to fill. |
>
> ---
>
> ## If you only have twenty minutes
>
> Any one conversation on page 5 stands alone. Pick by what your programme has
> not already covered:
>
> - **Conversation 1 — how you each say a hard thing.** Best if your session on
>   communication is coming up, or went flat.
> - **Conversation 2 — whose family is in the room.** The one most programmes
>   skip and most couples need. If you only ever run one of these, run this.
> - **Conversation 3 — what faith means here, in practice.** Works for couples
>   who share a tradition, couples who don't, and couples with none. The report
>   picks the right version.
> - **Conversation 4 — what you each think happens next.** Good near the end of a
>   programme, when the wedding is close and nobody has discussed the Wednesday
>   after it.
>
> Each has the question, why it was chosen for that couple, and a note on what a
> good answer sounds like.
>
> ---
>
> ## When it goes wrong
>
> **"This isn't me."** Good. Ask what it got wrong and what would be truer. A
> couple correcting the report are talking about the real thing, which is the
> point. The report is one day's self-report and it says so on page 8.
>
> **One of them goes quiet.** Do not fill it, and do not turn to the other for
> the answer. Ask the quiet one something factual and small before asking
> anything about feeling.
>
> **It turns into who is at fault.** Move to the concrete. Every conversation on
> page 5 has a version of *"what would you each do differently on Tuesday"* —
> specifics defuse what principles inflame.
>
> **Something serious surfaces.** Violence, coercion, an addiction, someone in
> real distress. Stop the exercise.
>
> This report cannot see any of that. It is twenty-two questions each of them
> answered about themselves on a good day, and it is not an assessment.
>
> **Refer through your own network.** We have no clinicians and we are not a
> referral service — the app can point anyone to external crisis lines and
> support services, free and without an account, but it cannot hand a couple to
> a professional and we will not pretend otherwise. Your judgement and your
> contacts are what covers this.
>
> Support resources are reachable from the app at any time, for anyone, with
> nothing behind a payment — ever.
>
> ---
>
> ## Showing the app in the room
>
> If you demonstrate the daily question, **show it with both answers still
> hidden**: *"you'll each answer this tonight, separately — it opens when you're
> both in."*
>
> Do not open a couple's real answers on a screen in front of the room. The
> mechanic is that nobody sees an answer until both have given one, and a spoiled
> reveal on a projector teaches everyone watching that the privacy is decorative.
>
> ---
>
> ## Two things we will not do, so you can tell them
>
> **We will not tell you what any couple answered.** Not the questionnaire, not
> anything they say in the app afterwards. You will see who has finished and who
> has not, so you can chase them, and nothing else. If we would build that for
> you, you would be right to wonder what we would tell someone else about your
> couples.
>
> **We will not flag couples to you as needing attention.** No risk scores, no
> "this pair needs a closer look". A system that quietly tells a third party a
> couple looks like a concern, on the strength of twenty-two questions they
> answered about themselves, is not something we would want pointed at us either.
> If something in the report worries someone, it goes to them.
>
> ---
>
> ## Why they take the assessment at home
>
> Because the questions are about how each of them handles closeness, distance
> and being let down — and nobody answers those honestly with the other person
> sitting beside them.
>
> That matters most for the couples it is hardest to see. Someone in a
> controlling relationship will not answer *"I worry about being abandoned"*
> truthfully with their partner watching, and that is exactly the couple you most
> need the report to be honest about.
>
> Pair them in the room. Let them answer at home.

## Notes on the draft

**Length.** One side of A4 at 10pt, or two at 11pt. If it grows past two sides it
stops being a guide and becomes something else nobody reads. The ninety-minute
table and the four failure modes are the parts to protect if it has to be cut.

**Register.** Written to a professional who has run this class before and does not
need to be taught how to facilitate. It tells them things only we know — what the
report can and cannot see, what we will not send them, and why the assessment
happens at home. It should never explain their own job to them.

**The refusals face the facilitator on purpose** (`refusals-as-proof.md`). Both
are real, both are enforced in code, and both answer objections a counsellor
raises unprompted. They belong in the room, not only on a web page.

**The at-home section is the clinical argument, not the logistics one.** A
counsellor recognises the controlling-dynamic point immediately, and it is the
reason the constraint reads as care rather than as inconvenience.

**Print and format.** Ships as a PDF alongside the cohort's reports, A4 and
Letter, and as plain text in the facilitator's confirmation email so it survives
being forwarded.
Programme branding on this page too, matching the report cover.

## Acceptance criteria

| # | Criterion |
|---|---|
| G.1 | Identical for every cohort; contains no couple's data of any kind. |
| G.2 | Fits two sides of A4 at 11pt, and prints legibly in greyscale. |
| G.3 | Ships with every cohort's report bundle and in the facilitator's confirmation email as plain text. |
| G.4 | Every capability it describes passes D3.16 — used in the running app, or covered by a passing test. It makes three claims (completion-only visibility, no risk flags, ungated support) and all three have tests behind them. |
| G.5 | Carries programme branding where supplied, consistent with the report cover. |

## Appendix — why the referral wording is what it is

*Added 2026-08-03 under D3.35 (no humans in the loop for v1), read together with
D3.28 (no report is generated for a couple in crisis, and the facilitator is
never told why).*

Put those two decisions side by side and a gap appears that neither creates
alone:

1. The software notices a couple is in a bad place and **declines to produce the
   artefact**.
2. It **tells the facilitator nothing** about why — correctly, since telling them
   is the risk-flagging we refused.
3. We have **no clinician to refer anyone to**.

So the facilitator's own referral network is not part of the safety net here.
**It is the whole of it.**

That makes "when it goes wrong" the only place in the product where that gap is
addressed, and it is why the wording says plainly that we have no clinicians
rather than leaving a helpful-sounding ambiguity. A facilitator who half-believes
we can hand a couple onward is worse off than one who knows the referral is
entirely theirs — they would be relying on something that does not exist, at the
moment it mattered most.

**Review severity:** this section is the mitigation for a deliberate product
silence, not guide copy. It should be reviewed at that bar, and any edit that
softens *"we have no clinicians"* into something warmer should be rejected on
sight.
